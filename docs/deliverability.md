# Deliverability

**Status:** operational policy. M1.4 (Sales agent — send path) implements what
is specified here. Where this document states a number, that number lives in
`config/base.yaml` and is read at runtime — never hardcoded in the send path.

This is not advice. Every rule below is either enforced in code or is a manual
step with a named owner. A rule that is neither does not belong in this file.

---

## 1. Domain and account architecture

Two domains, deliberately separate:

| Domain | Purpose | Reputation risk |
|---|---|---|
| `kimanimburu.com` | Brand site, normal business email | Must stay clean |
| `getkimani.com` | Cold outbound only | Expendable |

**Why separate.** Cold outreach accumulates spam complaints, bounces, and
filtering signals over time. If that happens on the domain hosting the site and
receiving client email, both degrade together — replies to real clients start
landing in spam, and the site's transactional mail suffers with it. A separate
sending domain contains the damage and makes it recoverable by replacing one
cheap domain.

**The sending account.** Cold outbound is sent from `kimani@getkimani.com`, a
Google Workspace mailbox on the sending domain (~$7/month). The Gmail API is
the send path, per the M0.3 decision to poll `history.list` rather than run
Pub/Sub.

**`kimanimburu098@gmail.com` is not a sending account and never will be.**
A free `@gmail.com` address cannot carry SPF, DKIM, or DMARC for a domain you
control, because you do not control `gmail.com`'s DNS. Every authentication
mechanism in section 2 is impossible from it. Google also filters cold outreach
from free accounts more aggressively, and a flagged personal account takes your
real correspondence down with it.

It has two legitimate uses, both listed later: a warmup seed address (§3) and a
weekly inbox-placement check (§9).

**Never send cold outreach from `kimanimburu.com`.** Enforced in code: the send
path refuses any `from` address whose domain is not the configured sending
domain, and fails loudly rather than silently falling back.

**The sending domain must resolve.** A prospect who receives cold mail will
sometimes type the domain into a browser. `getkimani.com` must redirect to
`kimanimburu.com`. A sending domain that resolves to nothing reads as throwaway
and invites a spam report. This matters more here than it would for a
`kimanimburu.co`, because `getkimani.com` does not contain the full name — the
redirect is what makes the connection obvious.

## 2. DNS records

Configured on `getkimani.com` at the registrar. All three are required before
the first send. Sending without them is the fastest way to land in spam.

### SPF
One TXT record at the root. Google Workspace is the only authorised sender.

```
Type:  TXT
Name:  @
Value: v=spf1 include:_spf.google.com ~all
```

There must be exactly **one** SPF record on the domain. Multiple SPF records is
a permanent fail, not a warning. A second sending service added later goes
inside this record as an additional `include:`, never as a second record.

### DKIM
Generated by Google Workspace, not hand-written. In the Admin console:
Apps → Google Workspace → Gmail → Authenticate email. Generate a 2048-bit key,
publish the TXT record it produces, then click **Start Authentication**.

```
Type:  TXT
Name:  google._domainkey
Value: (supplied by Google — long v=DKIM1 string)
```

DKIM is not active until "Start Authentication" is clicked. The DNS record
alone does nothing. This step is silently skipped often enough to call out.

### DMARC
Start permissive, tighten after two weeks of clean reports.

```
Type:  TXT
Name:  _dmarc
Value: v=DMARC1; p=none; rua=mailto:dmarc@kimanimburu.com; fo=1
```

`p=none` means monitor, do not act. Reports arrive at the `rua` address — note
it points at the brand domain, which you read regularly. After 14 days with no
unexpected sources appearing, move to `p=quarantine`. Do not start at
`p=reject`: a misconfiguration there destroys deliverability silently, with no
error surfaced anywhere.

### Verification
Before the first send, confirm all three resolve:

```bash
dig +short TXT getkimani.com
dig +short TXT google._domainkey.getkimani.com
dig +short TXT _dmarc.getkimani.com
```

Propagation is usually under an hour but can take 24. Do not begin warmup until
all three return the expected values.

## 3. Warmup

**Warmup is calendar time and cannot be compressed.** A new domain sending 40
messages on day one is treated as a spam source regardless of content quality.
This ramp gates M1.4 going live, not the code.

| Week | Daily cap | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 5 | Seed addresses only — addresses that will reply. |
| 2 | 10 | Begin real prospects. Keep seeding some replies. |
| 3 | 20 | Watch bounce rate closely. |
| 4 | 30 | |
| 5+ | 40 | Steady-state ceiling. |

**Replies matter more than volume during warmup.** A domain whose early
messages are opened and answered builds reputation far faster than one sending
into silence. Weeks 1–2 are not a formality — they are the mechanism.

**Seed addresses for weeks 1–2:**
- `kimanimburu098@gmail.com` (this is what the personal account is for)
- `kimanimburu.com` mailbox once it exists
- Mary, and anyone else who will reliably reply within a few hours

Reply to each seeded message from the receiving side. Two-way conversation is a
stronger signal than a one-way send that merely gets opened.

**40/day is the permanent ceiling on one mailbox.** This account represents one
person. A human does not send 200 cold emails a day, and sending like one is
the signal that gets a mailbox flagged. Scaling past 40 means additional
mailboxes on additional domains, each independently warmed — never raising this
number.

If sending stops for more than a week, resume at half the previous cap and ramp
back over several days. Reputation decays with inactivity.

## 4. Send caps and pacing

Enforced in code, read from `config/base.yaml`:

```yaml
deliverability:
  sending_domain: getkimani.com
  from_address: kimani@getkimani.com
  daily_cap: 5              # raised manually per the warmup table
  hourly_cap: 3
  min_gap_seconds: 180      # minimum spacing between sends
  jitter_seconds: 420       # random additional delay, 0..jitter
  send_window_start: "08:00"
  send_window_end: "17:00"
  send_days: [mon, tue, wed, thu, fri]
  timezone_source: recipient  # fall back to sender tz when unknown
```

**Pacing rules.**

- Never burst. Sends are spaced by `min_gap_seconds` plus random jitter. Twenty
  messages arriving in ninety seconds is a machine signature.
- Business hours only, in the recipient's timezone where known. A message
  arriving at 3am local is both ineffective and suspicious.
- Weekdays only. Weekend cold outreach converts poorly and reads as automated.
- The daily cap is a hard stop, not a target. Thirty queued messages against a
  cap of ten means twenty wait until tomorrow.

**The cap counts every outbound message on the domain**, follow-ups included.
A sequence at step 3 across 30 leads is 30 sends against the cap.

## 5. Address status tiers and suppression

### Verification and the three tiers

Every address is verified ONCE, at import (`integrations/email_verification.py`,
EmailListVerify), and the verdict is stored permanently on the contact. Credits
are charged per address and a mailbox's status does not go stale fast enough to
justify paying twice, so an address carrying any real verdict is never
re-verified — only an `unverified` contact is looked up. A verification that
fails (API down, out of credits, an unrecognised answer) leaves the contact
`unverified`, which is never-send: a failure can never produce a sendable
address.

The provider reports `role`, `disposable` and `accept_all` as booleans
*independent of* `status`. An address can be status `valid` AND role true, so
flags are evaluated first; tiering on status alone would send cold outreach to
`info@`.

| Tier | Statuses | Treatment |
|---|---|---|
| send | `valid` | Sends normally. |
| restricted | `catch_all` | Sendable, but capped at `catch_all_share` of `daily_cap` (0.4 → 2 of 5 today), and its bounces count double toward §6. |
| never | `unverified`, `risky`, `invalid`, `bounced`, `suppressed`, `disposable`, `role_based` | Never sent to, for any reason. |

Catch-all exists as its own tier because B2B domains at our ICP size frequently
run catch-all: refusing them outright would refuse most legitimate prospects.
But a catch-all domain accepts *everything*, so acceptance proves nothing about
the mailbox — permitted under stricter accounting, not treated as verified.

**`role_based` is never-send on two grounds, not one:** those addresses carry
bounce risk, *and* they land in shared inboxes where cold email is deleted
unread. The classification is correct for deliverability and for conversion.
Role addresses are caught twice over: by the verifier's `role` flag, and by a
local-part check (`role_based_local_parts`) that needs no credit.

Every `EmailStatus` must appear in exactly one tier or the config loader
refuses to boot — a status added later can never default to sendable by
omission.

### Suppression

Checked *before* every send, without exception. This is the most important
safety mechanism in the send path — a message to a suppressed address is worse
than no message at all.

**Permanent, never contactable again:**

- Anyone who unsubscribes, by any wording. "Take me off your list", "stop",
  "not interested, remove me". The reply classifier's `unsubscribe` intent
  writes to suppression immediately, before any other handling.
- Any hard bounce (invalid mailbox, domain does not exist).
- Any spam complaint.
- Anyone replying with hostility. The `escalation_signals` containing `anger`
  path suppresses and notifies a human. Do not attempt recovery.
- Manually added addresses and domains.

**Domain-level suppression.** One person unsubscribing suppresses their whole
company domain by default. Emailing a second person at a company that just
asked to be left alone is how a complaint becomes a report.

**Temporary:**

- Soft bounces: pause 7 days, retry once, then suppress permanently on a second
  failure.
- `not_now` with a resume date: paused until that date, not suppressed.
- Out-of-office: pause 7 days, and do not count as engagement.

Suppression is checked at send time against the live table, not at draft time.
A lead that unsubscribed between drafting and sending must not receive the
queued message.

## 6. Health thresholds and automatic pause

Computed on a rolling 7-day window, evaluated immediately before every send.

**Below 50 sends in the window, rates do not evaluate.** At warmup volume a rate is
noise — one bounce in 35 sends reads as 2.9% — so absolute counts apply instead:

| Below the sample floor | Hard pause |
|---|---|
| Hard bounces | 2 |
| Spam complaints | 1 |

**At or above 50 sends, the rates apply as written:**

| Metric | Warn | Hard pause |
|---|---|---|
| Bounce rate | 2% | 3% |
| Spam complaint rate | 0.05% | 0.1% |
| Unsubscribe rate | 2% | 5% |

The sample floor, the absolute counts and the rates all live in `config/base.yaml`
(`deliverability.health`). Bounce rate counts hard bounces only; soft bounces are
temporary suppressions (§5). A warn threshold alerts Slack at most once per metric per
day and does not pause.

**A bounce from a catch-all recipient counts double** (`bounce_weight_by_status`).
Acceptance from a catch-all domain never proved the mailbox existed, so the bounce is the
first real evidence about that address. Each bounce is weighted by the status the
recipient had *at the moment we reserved the send* (`messages.recipient_email_status`),
not by whatever the contact's status has become since. Combined with the floor above,
this means **one catch-all bounce inside the first fifty sends pauses sending on its
own** — deliberate: a false pause costs a day and one `scripts/resume_sending.py` run,
while a missed signal costs months of domain reputation.

**Hard pause stops the send path entirely** and alerts Slack. It does not
throttle, degrade, or continue at reduced volume. It stops, and a human decides
what happens next. This is deliberate: each of these crossing its threshold
means something is wrong with the list or the message, and continuing to send
makes an expensive problem worse.

Resuming requires a manual action and a recorded reason.

**Bounce rate is the leading indicator.** It almost always means list quality,
not content — addresses guessed, scraped, or years stale. The fix is the list,
never the copy.

## 7. Message-level requirements

Enforced by validators in the send path. A draft failing any of these is not
sent, and the failure is surfaced rather than silently corrected.

- **Plain text or minimal HTML.** No tracking pixels, no images, no marketing
  templates, no newsletter-styled footers. It should look like a person wrote
  it in Gmail, because a person nearly did.
- **No open tracking.** Apple Mail Privacy Protection makes open rates
  meaningless, and tracking pixels are a spam signal. Engagement is measured by
  replies and meetings only — which is why the scorer weights opens at zero.
- **Link discipline.** At most one link, only when it earns its place. Zero
  links in a first-touch message is preferable.
- **A working opt-out in every message.** Not a styled footer — a plain
  sentence offering to stop. Legally required under CAN-SPAM, good practice
  everywhere.
- **A real physical address** in the signature. CAN-SPAM requires it.
- **Under 120 words** for first touch, enforced by the code-computed word count.
- **No misleading subject lines.** No "Re:" on a first message, no fake
  threading, no implying a prior conversation that did not happen.

## 8. Legal basis

**United States (CAN-SPAM).** Cold B2B email is lawful. Requirements: accurate
header and sender information, a non-deceptive subject line, a functioning
opt-out honoured within 10 business days, and a valid physical postal address.
This system honours opt-outs immediately, not within 10 days.

**EU/UK (GDPR / PECR).** B2B outreach to a corporate address may rest on
legitimate interest, but the bar is higher: the message must be relevant to the
recipient's professional role, opt-out must be clear, and a record of the
lawful basis must exist. ICP targeting and the `problem_statement` requirement
partly serve this. UK sole traders are treated as consumers under PECR and are
out of scope.

**Practically:** targeting a named decision-maker at a company matching a
documented ICP, with a message about their professional responsibilities and a
clear opt-out, is defensible. Bulk-mailing scraped lists is not, anywhere.

A summary for operating purposes, not legal advice. Anything touching a
regulated sector, or a jurisdiction outside the US/UK/EU, gets checked before
sending.

## 9. Monitoring

**Google Postmaster Tools** — add and verify `getkimani.com`. The only direct
visibility into how Gmail sees the domain: spam rate, domain reputation,
authentication pass rates. Check weekly during warmup. Data appears only above
a volume floor, so expect it to be sparse early.

**DMARC reports** — arriving at `dmarc@kimanimburu.com`. Read for unexpected
sending sources, which indicate misconfiguration or spoofing.

**Weekly manual inbox check during warmup.** Send from `kimani@getkimani.com`
to `kimanimburu098@gmail.com` and to an Outlook and a Yahoo address. Confirm
inbox, not spam. This catches problems before Postmaster Tools has enough
volume to show them, and is the second legitimate use of the personal Gmail.

## 10. Pre-flight checklist

All must be true before M1.4 sends its first real message.

- [ ] `getkimani.com` registered
- [ ] `kimanimburu.com` registered and pointed at Netlify
- [ ] `getkimani.com` redirects to `kimanimburu.com`
- [ ] Google Workspace active on `getkimani.com`, mailbox `kimani@getkimani.com`
- [ ] SPF published, exactly one record, verified via `dig`
- [ ] DKIM key generated **and** authentication started in the Admin console
- [ ] DMARC published at `p=none` with a working `rua` address
- [ ] Google Postmaster Tools verified for `getkimani.com`
- [ ] Suppression table exists and is checked pre-send in code
- [ ] Health thresholds configured and the pause path tested
- [ ] Warmup week 1 complete, with replies actually received
- [ ] A real physical address available for the signature
